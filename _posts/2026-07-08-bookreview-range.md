Recently I read the book [**Range - Why generalists Triumph In a Specialized World**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range:_Why_Generalists_Triumph_in_a_Specialized_World) by David Epstein. It got my attention as I saw really good reviews about it and relevancy. Also becoming a generalist - as one of the strategies to deal with the rapid changes that AI brings about, is something that was brewing in my head for a while. So when I saw the reviews it rang a bell and really the stuff I was looking forward to learn more about.<br>

Why ? <br>

"AI is an multiplier" the ever so familar term these days,  is something I strongly experienced. I could do things now which were not possible earlier (due to entry moats) - like developing a [mobile app](https://nilanka-weeraman.github.io/welcome/). <br>
So the intuition was - broaden your knowledge, so that if you want to dwelve deeper, there is no better time than now. <br>
On the other hand, reflecting on how I started my career as an Engineer in telecom industry then moving to the field of data (before I knew it was [sexiest job in 20th century](https://hbr.org/2012/10/data-scientist-the-sexiest-job-of-the-21st-century)), reminds me nothing but how sometimes the diverse experience helped in finding patterns, bring over practices to other domains. Even in a field like data, there are multiple specializations - data analytics / science, data engineering, Ops ( ML/Data ). Being able to wear multiple hats is a blessing. The troubleshooting, investigating, triaging, planning skills I obtained during an Engineering career nicely overlaps with that of data field.  <br> And then there are other non-technical but equally important skills of how to manage situations, teams & working in teams are something equally or more imporatant than the tech dimensions. Large corporates, while building their leadership funnels, actively practice this by placing potential individuals in diverse funcational scopes. <br>

So I wanted to make sense of these observations and intuitions and thought to pick the brains of David Epstien.<br>

The book has a nice flow and very interesting to read. The author organized real world examples that suites the core argument. The author himself was relecting upon how it helped him being a wanderer or not to early-specialise into a career. He draws very interesting and inspiring real world examples, such as Roger Federer, Vincent van Gough. <br>

The book is filled with stories about how late specialisers thrived into successful careers. <br>
Other intersting mentions, which I can recollect now are ( I read the book about two months ago ) ; 
- too much grit could be counterproductive, which is surprising when you first hear it. The speaker and author of the famous [Ted talk](#https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=H14bBuluwB8&vl=en) on this apparently worried that her ideas were misinterpreted as per this book. _My take - deciding which paths to follow, which ones to give up - there is no formula, other than being optimisting and absorb things as they happen._
- systems thinking. Having the birds-eye view and interactions & implications of one action to another. Specially comes in handy when forecasting which the book covers with great examples and points you to different rabbit holes if you are really keen. <br>
- lateral thinking. How some labs were able to successfully borrow ideas from other fields to be innovative. A perfect example is , of course the field of AI and how labs like GoogleDeepmind are obsessed about neuroscience, world models, physics as a pathway to build general intelligence. ( The book doesn't cover any ai labs as it was written in 2017)
- some tips on career matching. How fast, quality feedback ( implicit ) helps to sample different pathways and alter course of action. <br>

I find every observation and argument to be facinating and inspiring in the book <br>

However in my own view, for an individual to be in a position to be in a cycle of doing , collecting feedback (implicit & reflective) is a luxury and many don't have the space or time. <br>
For example -  how it is becoming very difficult it is to get an internship in your field ( based on real-life experience as an hiring person ) <br>
Thus in my view there is an element of luck, randomness which can't be always explain. And this is something the influencial author Nassem Taleb wrote book about.
<br>

On a positive note, what stuck with me is the imporatance of broad fields like Economics which is an holistic field. Further the attempts by institutions ( as in the book ) to taught 'how to think systematically or laterally' via programs like computational thinking or integrated science programs. <br>

This complled me to take an online course called ['Model Thinking'](#https://www.coursera.org/learn/model-thinking) which briefs about various models or frameworks. The course is very high-level, yet packed with ideas from economics, social sciences etc. <br>

And yet, there are paradoxes as one of the people I follow on twitter - Nassen Taleb, often critisizing economists, social scientists, journalists, diplomats, government officials , academics, data scientists (oops!!) and saying that they don't have [skin in the game](#https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skin_in_the_Game_(book)) - a book I only could manage half way through. But I think I'm loosely getting his core idea, that some professions don't take accountability or risks as others does. That no amount of knowledge or theory only makes a person - genuine or have _skin in the game_ ( even though they might hold a executive position in a large corporate , or mine data (Oops again ) ). <br>
Again I filtered out (out of confirmation bias of course), what I understood by Tableb's argument and take anything inspiring from the book - Range and not to believe everything in the book or be awe of all the examples that's been laid out there and assuming late specialisation is the way forward. May be it is just randomness as Taleb puts out and we can't plan for late specialisation. <br>

But to give yourself a good chance of navigating in different paths one can attain or sharpen the skills such as broader thinking (systems thinking), ability to connect dots and being open ( lateral thinking to loosely put ) and just do things & learn ( have some skin in the game ) <br>

But if you are curious and like to go down in rabbit holes, the book might be a great read as it did for me.
<br>

And I hope you'd simply find this write-up as a wayward, poorly organized one, which it is. BUT if you are curious, there could be something for you.
